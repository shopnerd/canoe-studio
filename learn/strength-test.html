Thin-section strength test

Break thin strips and plates cast exactly like the hull, same thickness, same layers and mesh, on the bridge tester, converted to push on a concrete specimen instead of a model bridge. It gives the two numbers the structure check depends on, plus the broken flexural sample the display requires.

Why these two tests
  • Bending (flexural) strip: the hull wall bends between ribs, under water pressure and when the canoe flexes. This gives the first-crack stress (use it as Composite tensile strength in the app) and the peak stress (the “composite flexural strength” the Design Narrative reports, with its test age).
  • Punching plate: a paddler’s knee on a thin floor. This checks the app’s punching-shear estimate against a real break.
  • One broken flexural specimen goes on the display table (RFP §7.2.1).

Make the specimens

  1. Cast flat panels, not cylinders. Use the same mold release, the same concrete layers and thicknesses, and mesh placed exactly as in the hull (e.g. ⅛ in / mesh / ¼ in / mesh / ⅛ in). Place and trowel them the way you’ll do the canoe. The point is to test the composite, including your workmanship.
  2. Cure them like the hull, same method and time. Mark each panel with mix, layup, cast date and which face is the finish (troweled) side.
  3. Cut specimens with a wet saw (dust control) once they’re strong enough, or cast them to size in divided forms:
    • Bending strips: about 2 in wide × 12 in long. ASTM C947, the standard for thin-section glass-fiber concrete up to 1 in thick, uses third-point loading. Borrow the standard and match its specimen and span details if you’ll cite it.
    • Punching plates: about 9 × 9 in.
  4. Make enough. Five strips per mix and layup per test age (7, 14 and 28 days is a good set), plus three plates. Strength scatters, and one break isn’t data.
  5. Measure before breaking. Width and thickness with calipers at the middle and both load points. Thickness is squared in the formula, so a 10% thickness error is a 20% strength error.

Convert the bridge tester

A bridge tester pulls a loading block down. For bending, the specimen sits on two supports and something has to press down on top of it. A yoke does that: a top bar with two loading rollers rests on the specimen, and two threaded rods run down past the sides of the specimen to a bottom bar that the tester pulls. The supports sit on a rigid frame bolted to the tester, with a gap so the rods pass freely.

  • Capacity and resolution. A 2 in strip at hull thickness breaks at roughly 50–150 lb, and a punching plate at a few hundred. The tester’s load reading must resolve about 1 lb, and the yoke’s own weight has to be subtracted or tared.
  • Rate. Load smoothly, with no jerks, so the specimen fails in roughly one to three minutes. A crank or winch that jumps in steps gives low, scattered results.
  • Deflection is worth recording: a dial indicator under midspan, or phone video beside a ruler. Mesh-reinforced composites carry load well after first crack, and that toughness is a story worth telling.
  • Safety. Safety glasses and a clear shield. Thin concrete fails suddenly and throws chips.

Feed the results back

  • Canoe Studio › Rules & structure: set Composite tensile strength to the average first-crack stress, a conservative choice since the hull shouldn’t be designed to crack. Set f′c from cylinder breaks. The Rules tab’s bending and punching checks update immediately.
  • Design Narrative / infographic: report composite flexural strength (peak) with its age, e.g. “1,520 psi at 7 days” as Virginia Tech did in 2025.
  • Season report: predicted vs. measured strength, section 3.
  • Display: keep the best broken strip, labeled with its load, stress and age.
Formulas: third-point σ = P·L / (b·d²); center-point σ = 3·P·L / (2·b·d²); punching v = P / (b₀·d) with d ≈ t/2 and b₀ = 4(a + d), compared to ACI 318 two-way vc = 4√f′c (psi, λ = 1). These are simple-beam estimates for comparison. The competition rules don’t prescribe the flexural method, so state the method you used in the report.
