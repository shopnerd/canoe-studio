Mix & troweling notes

Working notes on the hardest practical problem: a lightweight mix that can be troweled onto the inside of the hull. They start from what the 2025 finalists published and the 2027 limits, and they’re meant to grow as the team adds its own test data.

Read this as hypotheses

These notes are general lightweight-mortar practice plus what other teams showed publicly, not a recipe. The team’s own materials, test results and mix lead decide.

First, redefine “workable”

Usual target

A mix that trowels to a smooth finish on the inside of the hull.

What the finalists actually needed

A mix that goes on at a set thickness, stays put on a steep wall, and bonds to the layer below for as long as placement takes. Smoothness comes later, from sanding.

Every raw layer on the 2025 cutaways was lumpy. Florida checked thickness with QC pins; Virginia Tech sanded 60 → 200 → 400 → 1000 grit. That changes what to measure: “troweling feel” is subjective, but sag on a vertical wall, flow loss over 45 minutes, and next-day layer bond are numbers.

Three things stand out. VT’s structural mix runs at 1 in slump: stiff enough to hold on the walls, with air, latex and retarder doing the work that water would otherwise do. Florida and UW-Madison ran two mixes, one for structure and one for the surface or for flowing into details. And glass bubbles (K25, K37, S38) sat in almost every jar lineup as the finest aggregate fraction.

What 2027 changes in the recipe

  • No hydrated lime or quicklime. Lime was a common cohesion and trowelability aid, so its job has to come from somewhere else: metakaolin or other fine pozzolans, a viscosity-modifying admixture (ASTM C494 Type S), or latex.
  • Hydraulic cement under 50% of the binder, and 30–50% of the binder must be ASTM C595 blended cement (e.g. Type IL). The rest is fly ash, slag, silica fume, metakaolin or glass pozzolan. High-SCM mixes set slowly: more open time, but slower early strength and later demolding.
  • Aggregate ≥ 35% of volume; oven-dried unit weight < 80.0 pcf; latex solids ≤ 100 lb/yd³ from the approved list; two mixes maximum. Full list in the rules guide.

Diagnose before changing anything

“Bad workability” covers several different problems with opposite fixes. Name the symptom first.

Tests that turn feel into numbers

ASTM C1437 · 0/15/30/45 min

Flow-loss curve

One flow table and a timer. Separates “too stiff to start” from “stiffens over time”, which need different fixes.

Team test · 15 min

Wall hang

Trowel a ⅜ in layer over mesh onto plywood at 60° and 90°. Measure sag and slump-off after 15 minutes. That’s the property the hull walls actually need.

Team test · next day

Layer bond

Two lifts placed 20 and 45 minutes apart on a scrap panel. Next day, try to pry them apart and record which gap failed.

ASTM C138 · every batch

Unit weight

Catches crushed bubbles and creeping water on the day, not at the weigh-in.

Borrowed from industry

Glass-fiber-reinforced concrete (GFRC) shops spray a thin, fiber-free face coat against the mold first, then build the fiber-rich backing behind it. That’s the same idea as UW-Madison’s ⅛ / ¼ / ⅛ split: one mix tuned for the surface, one for structure. It fits the two-mix limit.

Questions for the mix lead

  1. Is the problem initial stiffness, loss over time, sagging or stickiness? Is there a flow-loss curve yet?
  2. Are the lightweight aggregates pre-soaked, and is that moisture counted in the water?
  3. What replaces hydrated lime’s cohesion under the 2027 rules: VMA, metakaolin or latex?
  4. How long does placing one full layer take, and how big are the batches relative to that?
  5. Would a dedicated face-layer mix plus a structural mix be simpler than one mix that does everything?
